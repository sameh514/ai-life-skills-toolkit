# Lesson principles and review rubric

Use this reference for new decks, major rewrites, and presentation-quality reviews. The source notes preserve where each lesson came from; the synthesis turns them into flexible operating guidance.

## Source lessons

### Carl Kwan: The Do's and Don'ts of Effective Presentation Slides

Source: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tEF2vNP3S9A>

- 00:42–01:08: Clarity includes legibility. Use type large enough for distant viewers and high-resolution images rather than fuzzy assets.
- 01:08–01:59: Minimal text is not a universal goal. Audience needs may justify more visible guidance, including for non-native speakers or people who need help tracking the talk.
- 01:59–03:07: Prefer phrases and keywords to sentences. If points are sequential, introduce them with the speaker rather than showing the full list at once.
- 02:35–03:35: A dense Word-document paste makes a poor presentation because the audience reads faster than the presenter can speak, encouraging verbatim delivery.
- 03:35–04:00: A presentation is not a report. Slides should guide the audience through the talk.

### Harvard Business Review: How to Make a Presentation Deck that Doesn't Stink

Source: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sQzD3st8mTk>

- 00:47–01:35: A presentation is a show: the audience should learn while staying engaged with the speaker.
- 01:35–02:20: The central design skill is organizing content and information. Simple, well-organized work beats beautiful but disorganized work.
- 02:20–02:43: Common overload comes from too many or irrelevant photos, colors, and typefaces. Simplicity improves clarity.
- 02:43–03:28: For ordinary live pacing, target about 30 seconds and usually no more than one minute per slide. A slide requiring four or five minutes often contains an idea that should be broken into smaller pieces.
- 03:28–04:02: The presenter's authority, organization, engagement, humor, and passion are all legitimate forms of personality; tune their proportions to the audience.
- 04:02–04:45: Audience is the key design question. Understand why people are present, what they need, their background, and the communication style of important stakeholders.
- 04:45–05:35: Start with a text outline, then test pacing and top-level points before adding visuals. Keep takeaways visible; use visuals for secondary ideas when they amplify rather than repeat.
- 05:35–07:33: Color and animation become more powerful when used less. Let content drive them, keep motion simple, and apply accent colors consistently so viewers learn their meaning.
- 07:33–09:25: The demonstration reinforces five tactics: outline first, time the slides, use meaningful supporting visuals, write clear concise guidance instead of a script, and design around the audience.

The videos teach decision principles, not a visual template. Do not reproduce their literal yellow, pink, black, or teal treatments unless those choices also fit the user's brief.

## Resolve the text-density tension

"Use less text" and "give the audience enough text" are compatible when density follows the delivery mode.

### Live, speaker-led

- Show the takeaway, key labels, and essential evidence.
- Put explanation and nuance in speaker notes.
- Favor 30–60 second units and progressive sequencing when the order matters.

### Virtual or hybrid

- Keep text large for small screens and leave extra breathing room around critical labels.
- Use slightly more explicit headings and transitions because attention is easier to lose.
- Do not assume animation or fine visual detail will transmit reliably.

### Self-paced or leave-behind

- Add the context required to understand each slide without narration.
- Use short explanatory statements, source/method notes, and an appendix instead of a pasted report.
- Preserve a claim-first hierarchy so skimming still reveals the argument.

If one deck must serve both live and leave-behind uses, keep the live slide concise and put additional detail in notes or an appendix. Create a denser companion version only when the user actually needs one.

## Slide-map fields

Use a compact table or structured object with these fields:

- `number`
- `purpose`
- `audience_question`
- `takeaway`
- `visible_copy`
- `evidence`
- `visual_role`
- `speaker_notes`
- `target_time_seconds`
- `layout_archetype`

The map is an internal production artifact. Do not expose it on audience-facing slides.

## Review rubric

Score each dimension 0, 1, or 2. A 0 requires revision before delivery; a 1 requires a conscious tradeoff or targeted improvement.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audience fit | Generic or wrong audience | Audience named but unevenly served | Content, tone, examples, and detail fit the stated audience |
| Purpose | No clear outcome | Purpose appears but drifts | Deck consistently drives the intended takeaway or decision |
| Narrative | Information dump | Mostly ordered, with weak transitions | Clear opening, progression, and ending |
| Slide jobs | Several major ideas per slide | A few mixed-purpose slides | Each ordinary slide has one clear job |
| Pacing | Long static stretches or rushed density | Mostly workable with a few exceptions | Deliberate rhythm; complex ideas are sensibly segmented |
| Visible copy | Report prose, tiny text, or no guidance | Understandable but wordy or sparse | Concise, readable guidance appropriate to delivery mode |
| Visual meaning | Decorative, irrelevant, blurry, or misleading | Useful but inconsistent | Every visual clarifies, amplifies, or supplies evidence |
| Design restraint | Too many colors, fonts, effects, or elements | Mostly controlled with occasional noise | Simple system with consistent hierarchy and color semantics |
| Presenter relationship | Slides duplicate the speech | Some complementary content | Slides and speaker deliberately complement each other |
| Technical quality | Overlap, clipping, broken assets, or unresolved placeholders | Minor visible defects | Clean full-size renders and coherent deck-level contact sheet |

## Final review questions

- Can a first-time viewer state the deck's main point after scanning the slide titles?
- Does each title or focal statement express a takeaway rather than merely naming a topic?
- Is any slide functioning as a pasted document page?
- Can the smallest important text be read in the actual viewing context?
- Would removing any image, color, icon, animation, or effect improve clarity? If yes, remove it.
- Does each repeated accent color mean the same thing?
- Is any ordinary slide expected to remain unchanged for several minutes without a deliberate reason?
- Are the most important facts, charts, and visual assets sourced in the speaker notes?
- Has every slide been inspected at full size after the final edit?
